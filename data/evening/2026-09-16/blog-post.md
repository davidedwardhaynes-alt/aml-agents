# Singapore attacks scams from both ends, and a trade registry shows its blind spot

*Alex and Jordan on Singapore's new anti-scam law and AI pilot, a trade finance fraud exposing registry gaps, VTB sanctions, and private equity in Asian insurers.*

Tonight Alex and Jordan trace one theme through four stories: financial crime lives in the gaps between institutions, and the question is whether anyone is actually closing them. Singapore is trying, hard, from two directions at once. A trade finance case shows what happens when a fix built for one scandal doesn't cover the next. And two further stories — VTB sanctions and private equity in Asian insurance — show the same blind-spot problem playing out in sanctions screening and prudential oversight.

## Singapore's two-track assault on scams

MAS is testing AI models on shared, cross-bank data with a law enforcement agency to catch scam fund flows in near real time, because, as MD Chia Der Jiun put it in Mumbai, a single bank only sees a slice of the transaction chain. Findings are due by year end; MAS hasn't named the participating banks.

Separately, Singapore passed the Scams Countermeasures Bill on 9 September. Police can now issue a disclosure order, an account disabling order (up to thirty days, extendable once) and a service limitation order (up to three years) on reasonable suspicion — a lower bar than existing powers, deliberately, so action can come before the scam completes rather than after. Platform fines rise from SGD 1 million to SGD 10 million per breach, now the highest fixed penalty in the statute book. The law answers a real number: ninety scam reports a day, roughly SGD 2 million lost daily, SGD 410.6 million lost in the first half of this year alone.

Jordan rates the law as the more consequential move; Alex pushes back on one point, calling thirty-day account disabling on mere suspicion a significant power over someone's ability to pay rent, appeal mechanisms notwithstanding, and wants to see how it runs in month one before calling it a success. Jordan, in turn, won't call the AI pilot a breakthrough yet — it's untested and unnamed — but agrees the diagnosis is right: fraud dies in the gaps between institutions.

## Radiant World and the registry that doesn't check what you'd assume

Singapore's Trade Finance Registry was built after Hin Leong specifically to stop duplicate financing, and Jordan says it works for that. It does nothing to verify whether the underlying invoice is genuine, and doesn't connect to any equivalent registry elsewhere.

That gap is now on display in the Radiant World case, reported in detail by Regulation Asia. A Jefferies-run fund has a freezing injunction in Singapore covering up to USD 499.2 million, on top of a worldwide freezing order from London where the founder's personal spending is capped at GBP 4,000 a week. Incomlend is separately suing for over USD 34 million, alleging Radiant used Glencore invoices already paid or not genuine at all. Mizuho is in for roughly USD 100 million against invoices Glencore says it doesn't recognise, and is now pushing the Singapore operating company into judicial management, with the next hearing on the 23rd. MAS confirms banks must file STRs on suspicious activity but won't say whether any have been filed on this case.

Jordan's line: he's watched registries get built to fix the last scandal and miss the next one. This is that.

## VTB sanctions and a compliance deadline with no grace period

OFAC has sanctioned Russia's VTB Bank under its Iran authorities as part of Operation Economic Outcast, and named VTB's branches in Shanghai, Beijing and New Delhi explicitly in the designation — unusual for OFAC, and, Alex argues, deliberate: it's aimed at making sure Chinese and Indian banks see it. VTB was already heavily sanctioned over Ukraine; this adds a separate Iran-specific tag on the SDN List. Treasury says VTB built correspondent ties to sanctioned Iranian banks and moved billions in frozen Iranian assets through a rial-ruble settlement system.

Any bank in the region with a VTB correspondent relationship is now screening against two separate sanctions programmes for the same counterparty, and Alex says this isn't a case for waiting on enforcement. Jordan goes further, predicting an enforcement action against someone in Asia within the year, given how specifically the branches were named.

## Private equity's growing footprint in Asian insurers

HKMA research, also covered by Regulation Asia, finds private equity ownership of Asia Pacific insurers has risen from 24 to 36 percent of the sample over a decade. PE-owned insurers put 13 percent of assets into private credit against 8 percent for others, rising to 17 percent where PE holds a majority stake — and that credit is both larger and murkier, with 62 percent sitting in funds and structured products versus 48 percent elsewhere, and 68 percent of that in distressed and other risky strategies against 41 percent elsewhere.

Alex reads HKMA's careful language — the allocation to opaque assets rises eight percentage points two years after a PE investment, seventeen points within a year for majority stakes — as a regulator building a case file ahead of a rule, not banning anything yet. Jordan's summary is blunter: the policyholder's premium is quietly getting riskier and nobody has told them.
