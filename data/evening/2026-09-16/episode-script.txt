Singapore Attacks Scams From Both Ends At Once
Singapore pairs a cross-bank AI pilot with sweeping new scam legislation, while a Singapore trade finance fraud and fresh Iran sanctions on VTB Bank test compliance teams across the region.

ALEX: Evening. There's a thread running through tonight that's worth naming up front — how much of financial crime happens in the seams between institutions, the bit no single bank can see. Singapore's spending the day trying to close that gap from two directions. And then there's a trade finance case doing the same thing, badly, in the other direction.

JORDAN: Right, so let's start with Singapore, because they've genuinely moved on two fronts in the same week. First, the Monetary Authority of Singapore is testing AI models on shared, cross-bank data with a law enforcement agency, to catch scam fund flows in near real time. Chia Der Jiun said the quiet part out loud in Mumbai — a single bank only sees a slice of the transaction chain. Findings are expected by the end of this year.

ALEX: And separately, they've actually passed a law. The Scams Countermeasures Bill, through on the ninth of September.

JORDAN: This is the one I actually rate. Police can now issue a disclosure order, an account disabling order — up to thirty days, extendable once — and a service limitation order, up to three years, on nothing more than reasonable suspicion. That's a lower bar than existing powers, deliberately, so they can act before the scam happens rather than after.

ALEX: Ninety scam reports a day, about two million Singapore dollars lost — every single day. Four hundred and eleven million lost in the first half of this year alone. That's the number the law's actually answering.

JORDAN: And the platform fines went from one million Singapore dollars to ten million, per breach. That's now the highest fixed penalty in the statute book. If you're a designated online platform in Singapore and your anti-scam controls are still theatre, that number just became real.

ALEX: I'll push back slightly on one thing — account disabling on suspicion, for thirty days, is a big power to hand police over someone's ability to pay rent. They've built in appeal mechanisms. But I'd want to see how that actually runs in month one before calling it a success.

JORDAN: Fair. And I won't call the AI pilot a breakthrough either — it's a test, MAS hasn't even named the banks. But structurally it's the right diagnosis. Fraud dies in the gaps between institutions.

ALEX: Which is almost exactly the story Regulation Asia's been tracking with Radiant World.

JORDAN: This is Singapore's iron ore trader, and it's a mess. Jefferies-run fund's got a freezing injunction in Singapore covering up to four hundred and ninety-nine million US dollars, on top of a worldwide freezing order out of London — the founder's personal spending is capped at four thousand pounds a week, which tells you how seriously London's taking it. Separately, Incomlend's suing for over thirty-four million, saying Radiant used Glencore invoices that were already paid, or not genuine at all.

ALEX: And Mizuho's in for around a hundred million, secured against invoices Glencore says it doesn't recognise. They're now trying to put the Singapore operating company into judicial management. Next hearing's the twenty-third.

JORDAN: Here's the bit that should worry every trade finance desk listening. Singapore built a Trade Finance Registry after Hin Leong, specifically to stop duplicate financing. It works — for duplication. It does nothing to verify whether the underlying invoice is real. And it doesn't talk to any equivalent registry in another jurisdiction.

ALEX: So the same document could, in theory, finance itself twice over, once in Singapore and once somewhere the registry can't see.

JORDAN: That's the allegation, essentially. MAS says banks have to file STRs where they see suspicious activity, but won't confirm whether they've actually received any on this one. I've sat in the room when a registry gets built to fix the last scandal and doesn't touch the next one. That's this.

ALEX: Right, let's move to something with a harder deadline attached, because this one's a genuine compliance headache from tomorrow morning. OFAC has sanctioned Russia's VTB Bank under its Iran authorities — Operation Economic Outcast — and they didn't do it quietly. The designation notice names VTB's branches in Shanghai, Beijing, and New Delhi, specifically.

JORDAN: Sorry — why name the branches? That's not normal OFAC style.

ALEX: Because they want China and India's banks to see it. VTB was already one of the most heavily sanctioned banks on earth over Ukraine. This adds an Iran-specific tag — IRAN-EO13902 — on the SDN List, on top of everything already there. Treasury says VTB built correspondent relationships with sanctioned Iranian banks and moved billions in frozen Iranian assets, plus a settlement system in rials and rubles.

JORDAN: So if you're a bank in Shanghai or New Delhi with any VTB correspondent relationship —

ALEX: You're now screening against two separate sanctions programmes for the same counterparty, and OFAC has told you in plain English to cut the relationship off. This is not a case for waiting to see if enforcement follows. Treasury's already meeting global institutions to walk them through it.

JORDAN: Given how specifically they named those branches, I'd be surprised if this doesn't produce an enforcement action against someone in Asia within the year.

ALEX: Which brings me to something quieter, but I think more consequential over a longer horizon. Hong Kong's Monetary Authority has published research on private equity ownership of insurers across Asia Pacific, and it's not comfortable reading. Private equity ownership of the region's insurers has gone from twenty-four percent to thirty-six percent of the sample over a decade.

JORDAN: And that matters because?

ALEX: Because it changes what the insurer does with your premium. Insurers with private equity ownership put thirteen percent of assets into private credit, against eight percent for everyone else. Where private equity holds a majority stake, that's seventeen percent. And it's not just more — it's opaquer. Sixty-two percent of that private credit sits in funds and structured credit versus forty-eight percent for non-owned insurers, and within that, sixty-eight percent goes into genuinely risky strategies, distressed debt and the like, against forty-one percent elsewhere.

JORDAN: So the policyholder's premium is quietly getting riskier and nobody's told them.

ALEX: That's the finding, and HKMA's own language is careful — an insurer's allocation to the opaque stuff rises by eight percentage points two years after a private equity investment, seventeen points within a year if it's a majority stake. They're not banning anything. They're flagging that this "warrants closer monitoring." I'd read that as regulators building a case file before they build a rule.
